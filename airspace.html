1 · Understand

Key concepts

Airspace is controlled (classes A to E: ATC gives clearances) or uncontrolled (F and G). Going from A to G, ATC does less and you do more yourself. A class says three things: who may fly there, what ATC does for you, and what you must do: get a clearance, talk on the radio, keep the VMC minima.

The two SERA tables: airspace classes and VMC minima
Deep dive · how the pieces fit

Controlled airspace has names as well as classes. A control zone (CTR) starts at the surface and protects arrivals and departures. A control area (CTA) starts at a lower limit above the ground; a terminal control area (TMA) is a CTA where routes meet around one or more busy aerodromes, and an airway is a CTA shaped as a corridor. Each one is given a class by the State and published in the AIP.

Limits are part of the airspace. A volume is described by its lateral boundary and by its lower and upper limit, for example “3500 ft – FL195”. Below the lower limit you are not in it. Lower limits step up away from the aerodrome, so VFR flights can stay under them. At and below the transition altitude limits are altitudes on QNH; above it they are flight levels on 1013.25 hPa.

Special areas sit on top of the class. Prohibited (P) and restricted (R) areas may not be entered except under their published conditions or with permission; a danger area (D) warns of activity that may be dangerous to aircraft. An RMZ adds a radio requirement and a TMZ a transponder requirement inside airspace of any class they are placed in.

      3 · Key points

      Key points

      1The class decides what you need

      In A to D every flight needs a clearance and a radio. In E only IFR does: a VFR flight may fly there without talking to anyone. In F and G nobody needs a clearance. Separation follows the same pattern: the further down the alphabet, the more of it is your own job.

      2Read the limits, not only the lines

      A line on the chart is only part of the story. A TMA that starts at 3500 ft is not there at 3000 ft, so VFR traffic flies under it in class G. Know the lower limit at every point of your route, and which are altitudes and which are flight levels.

      3VFR is airspace and weather together

      The VMC minima change with altitude and class: 8 km above FL100, 5 km below, and clear of cloud with the surface in sight only low in F and G. Into a CTR you need a ceiling of 1500 ft and 5 km, or a special VFR clearance.

      Pilot takeaways

      • Plan the vertical profile, not only the line. Mark the lower limit of every controlled volume along the route and plan a level with a margin below it. An altimeter set wrong by 10 hPa is 300 ft wrong.
      • Call before the boundary, not at it. A clearance into a CTR or TMA is needed before you cross the line. Plan a holding point outside, and if you do not have the clearance, stay out.
      • Class E is shared airspace. IFR traffic there is separated only from other IFR. VFR flights may be there without a clearance or a radio, and often without a transponder. Keep a good lookout, and ask for traffic information when you can.
      • Check the special areas on the day. Restricted, danger and temporary areas are switched on and off by NOTAM and AIP times. An R area that was inactive yesterday can be active today.
      • Below the CTR minima, ask early. With a ceiling under 1500 ft or visibility under 5 km, a VFR arrival or departure needs special VFR, by day, clear of cloud with the surface in sight. Below 600 ft or 1500 m ATC will not give it.
      Deep dive · in the airliner

      Inside the system all the way. An IFR airliner is in controlled airspace from the runway to the runway: the CTR, the TMA on the SID, airways or upper airspace in cruise, and back down through the TMA on the STAR. In class C it is separated from everyone; in D and E only from other IFR traffic, so the crew still looks out and uses TCAS.

      Speed. The 250 kt limit below FL100 applies to IFR in classes D to G, not in C. Published SIDs and STARs and ATC instructions often restrict the speed anyway, so read the chart and the clearance.

      Leaving controlled airspace. Some regional and island aerodromes lie in class G with an RMZ instead of a CTR. An IFR approach there gets flight information, not ATC separation: listen out, give position reports, and expect VFR traffic that is not separated from you.

      Model and limitations. A fictional airspace for learning, not a real AIP. Heights are stretched low down in the pictures (not to scale) so the CTR and the TMA steps can be read. Flat terrain at sea level, so 3000 ft AMSL is also 1000 ft above the ground, and a transition altitude of 5000 ft. The rules are from the EASA rules of the air, SERA (Implementing Regulation (EU) No 923/2012): Appendix 4 for the classes, SERA.5001 and Table S5-1 for the VMC minima, SERA.5005 for VFR (CTR minima, night, FL195), SERA.5010 for special VFR, SERA.6005 for RMZ and TMZ and SERA.3145 for restricted areas. Reduced visibility allowed by the authority in classes F and G, and national rules, are not modelled. Always check the AIP and NOTAM for the real airspace.
