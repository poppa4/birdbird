Flight controls

Drag either control wheel on the panel, use the keyboard, a gamepad, the touch stick or this pad.

How to use the panel
Look around

Drag anywhere that is not a control to turn your head; scroll or pinch to zoom. The view buttons in the corner jump to the panel, the side windows, the pedestal and straight ahead. Click a part of the panel to look at it, Look ahead (or C, Esc) to face forward. Keys 0–9 look at panel areas; U, O, I, K (or the numpad) look left, right, up and down. Drag a control wheel to fly.

Switches and knobs

Click a rocker or toggle to flip it. Push-pull knobs (carburettor heat, primer, cabin heat and air, parking brake) toggle in and out with a click. Drag the throttle and mixture down to pull them out, up to push them in.

Ignition key

Click the key to step OFF → R → L → BOTH. Press and hold on START (or hold an arrow key at BOTH) to crank; it springs back to BOTH when you let go.

Radios and instrument knobs

Scroll on a knob or drag across it. On the double frequency knobs the outer ring changes MHz and the centre (or Shift) the decimals; ⇆ swaps standby and in-use. OBS knobs set the course, the altimeter knob the pressure setting.

How the model works

Which Cessna 172?

A typical classic C172: the carburetted 160 hp 172P-style aeroplane found at many clubs and schools, with a fixed-pitch propeller, flaps 0–30°, a split ALT/BAT master and a LOW VOLTAGE light. No autopilot (most trainers have none) and no rudder trim. On this model the fuel shutoff is the OFF position of the fuel selector; the separate red shutoff knob belongs to later fuel-injected 172s.

Engine start

A cold engine needs 2–3 primer strokes; a warm one little or none — over-priming floods it. Mixture rich, throttle open about ¼ inch, master on, clear the propeller, key to START. Release the key as soon as it fires and look for oil pressure within 30 seconds.

Carburettor ice

Fuel evaporating in the venturi cools the air by up to about 20 °C, so ice can form in humid air even on a warm day — worst at reduced power. First sign: a slow RPM drop with a fixed-pitch propeller, then rough running. Carb heat: full hot. The engine runs rougher and loses more RPM while the ice melts, then recovers.

Mixture and EGT

The carburettor runs richer as the air thins. Lean in cruise: pull the mixture slowly until the EGT peaks, then enrichen to about 50 °F rich of peak. Too lean: rough running and power loss. Full rich for take-off and landing at low-level aerodromes.

Magnetos

Two independent magnetos, each firing one plug per cylinder. At 1,700 RPM each magneto alone gives a small drop: max 125 RPM, max 50 RPM difference. No drop means a magneto that may not be grounding (a hot propeller); a big drop with rough running is a fouled plug or a faulty magneto.

Electrical

28 V alternator, 24 V battery. The ammeter shows battery current: charge after start, near zero in cruise, discharge if the alternator is off. LOW VOLTAGE comes on below about 24.5 V. Cycle the master (both halves) to reset the alternator control unit; if it comes on again, ALT off, shed load and land.

Vacuum and gyros

The engine-driven vacuum pump spins the attitude and heading gyros (suction 4.5–5.4 in Hg). After a pump failure they slow down over minutes: the attitude indicator slowly tilts and the heading indicator wanders while looking believable. The turn coordinator is electric. Cover the failed instruments and fly partial panel.

Magnetic compass

Dip makes the compass lag when turning off north and lead when turning off south (northern hemisphere), and show a turn towards north when you accelerate on an east or west heading. Read it in steady, wings-level flight.

What is simplified

The flight model is a generic light single tuned to typical C172 numbers. No P-factor or torque on the ground, no weight and balance, one fixed weight (about 2,200 lb). Gauges and limits are typical values for learning, not certified data.
