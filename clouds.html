1 · Understand

Key concepts

Rising air expands and cools. Whether it keeps rising depends on how fast it cools compared with the air around it: that is stability. Where it cools to its dew point, cloud forms; where the ground cools the air to its dew point, fog forms. Each card links to the place in the model where it is shown.

Deep dive · the numbers behind it

Why 400 ft per °C? Rising unsaturated air cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate, about 3 °C per 1000 ft, while its dew point falls only about 0.5 °C per 1000 ft. The spread closes by about 2.5 °C per 1000 ft, so each °C of spread is about 400 ft of lift (meteorologists use about 125 m per °C).

Why the saturated rate varies. Condensation releases latent heat. Warm air holds much more water vapour, so near the ground in summer the saturated rate is about 1.2–1.5 °C per 1000 ft; at 0 °C about 1.8; in the cold upper troposphere it approaches the dry rate. Exam questions often use about 1.8 °C per 1000 ft as an average.

ISA. The standard atmosphere uses 1.98 °C per 1000 ft (6.5 °C per km) up to the tropopause at 36 090 ft, where it is −56.5 °C. A real day’s profile is measured by radiosondes and drawn on a tephigram or skew-T chart.

    3 · Key points

    What happened, and why it matters

    1Stability decides the cloud

    A lifted parcel keeps rising only while it is warmer than the air around it. Stable air gives flat layers (ST, SC, NS), haze and smooth air; unstable air gives heaped cloud (CU, TCU, CB), showers and turbulence, and with enough depth and moisture, thunderstorms.

    2The base comes from the spread

    Rising air closes the temperature–dew point spread by about 2.5 °C per 1000 ft, so the base of convective cloud is about 400 ft per °C of spread. It works for cumulus, not for layer cloud brought in by a front.

    3Fog needs the spread to close at the ground

    Radiation fog: a clear sky, light wind and moist air at dusk. Advection fog: moist air over a colder surface, in stronger wind and at any time of day. The spread trend in the METAR warns you first.

    Pilot takeaways

    • Read stability in the METAR and TAF. CU, TCU, CB and SH mean unstable air: bumpy below the base, good visibility between showers. ST, SC, BR, FG and HZ mean stable air: smooth, but poor visibility and low ceilings.
    • Know the freezing level. In cloud between 0 °C and about −20 °C expect icing, worst in the tops of cumuliform cloud with large drops. In the A320: engine anti-ice in icing conditions, wing anti-ice when ice builds.
    • CB and TCU are avoided, not penetrated. Use the weather radar early and plan to stay well clear (typically 20 NM for a CB, more above the freezing level and downwind of the anvil).
    • Watch the spread in the evening. A spread of 2–3 °C at dusk with a clear sky and light wind means fog by dawn is likely. Check the destination and alternate TAFs, the RVR trend and your low-visibility minima.
    • Slant visibility can be far worse than the reported visibility. In shallow fog you may see the runway from above, then lose it on short final as you enter the layer.
    Deep dive · in the A320

    Fog and autoland. Fog is where the A320 autoland earns its keep. With the right approval, crew training and runway, CAT II and CAT III approaches go down to a decision height of 100 ft or less and RVRs of a few hundred metres. Check the RVR trend and the take-off minima before departure, and plan an alternate outside the fog area.

    Convective days. Plan the climb and descent around the CBs, tilt the radar to scan below the freezing level where the cell is most reflective, and expect turbulence and wind shear under and near the base of any large cumulus.

    3 · Test yourself

    Test yourself

    Model and limitations. Teaching model of one rising parcel. The dry adiabatic lapse rate is 3 °C per 1000 ft and the parcel’s dew point falls 0.5 °C per 1000 ft, so the cloud base is (T − Td) × 400 ft. The saturated lapse rate is computed from the temperature and the ISA pressure at each height (about 1.2–2.8 °C per 1000 ft). The air around has one straight lapse rate up to a tropopause at 36 000 ft (with heating, the layer below the cloud base is well mixed), with an optional inversion. There is no mixing with the air around, no ice phase and no moisture aloft, so real clouds are often smaller and real soundings are never straight lines. The cloud-type heights are typical mid-latitude values. The fog model uses typical cooling rates and wind and cloud effects and is illustrative only. Use the TAF, the METAR trend and the forecast charts for decisions.
