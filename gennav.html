The 1 in 60 rule

One degree of track error puts you about one nautical mile off track for every 60 NM flown, because sin 1° is close to 1/60. That turns off-track distances into angles in your head, and it holds well up to about 20°.

The Earth and directions

Great circle

A circle whose plane passes through the centre of the Earth. The equator and every meridian are great circles. It is the shortest route between two points, and its true track changes all the way, except along a meridian or the equator.

Rhumb line

A line that crosses every meridian at the same angle, so the true track is constant. Parallels of latitude are rhumb lines. It always lies on the equator side of the great circle, so it is longer.

Convergency and conversion angle

Convergency is the angle between two meridians, on the Earth about dlong × sin(mean latitude). The great-circle track changes by the convergency between the two ends. The conversion angle is half of it: the angle between the great circle and the rhumb line at each end.

conv = dlong × sin(mean lat) · CA = ½ conv
Departure

The east–west distance along a parallel. One degree of longitude is 60 NM at the equator, 30 NM at 60° N and nothing at the pole.

dep (NM) = dlong (min) × cos lat
True and magnetic

Charts and the great-circle formulas give true directions. The compass and most cockpit displays use magnetic. Variation east, magnetic least; variation west, magnetic best. Over mainland Norway the variation is currently a few degrees east.

M = T − var E (+ var W)

Time, longitude and daylight

The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, so 15° of longitude is one hour and 1° is four minutes. Local mean time follows the mean sun over your own meridian; UTC is the local mean time at Greenwich. Sunrise, sunset and twilight depend on latitude and date, and in Norway they range from polar night to midnight sun.

PET and PNR

The point of equal time (PET, or critical point) is where it takes as long to continue as to turn back. The point of safe return (PNR or PSR) is the furthest you can go and still get back with the reserves intact. Both depend on the wind; only the PNR depends on fuel.

PET formula

D is the total distance, O the groundspeed continuing and H the groundspeed returning (home), both at the TAS for the failure case. The time to the PET is that distance flown at the normal cruise groundspeed.

PET = D × H / (O + H)
PNR formula

E is the safe endurance: fuel available above the reserves divided by the fuel flow. O and H are the normal groundspeeds out and home.

T(PNR) = E × H / (O + H) · PNR = T × O
What the wind does

The PET moves into wind: a headwind on the way out moves it towards the destination. Any wind, head or tail, brings the PNR closer to the departure, because you lose more time on the slow leg than you gain on the fast one.

Teaching model. Spherical Earth with a radius of 3440 NM, no ellipsoid. Mach to TAS uses the ISA temperature lapse plus your deviation. Sunrise and twilight use a simple solar declination formula and ignore the equation of time (up to about 16 minutes), so times can be several minutes off. A320 speeds, fuel flows and failure-case TAS are rounded, illustrative teaching values, not FCOM data. Use the operational flight plan, the FMS and the operations manual for real planning.
