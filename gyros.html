The magnetic compass and its errors

The compass magnets hang from a pivot and try to line up with the earth’s field. That field points down into the ground at high latitudes (dip), so the assembly is built with its centre of gravity off the pivot. Accelerate or turn and that offset makes the card swing: the compass shows a heading you are not flying.

True, magnetic, compass

True heading minus easterly variation gives magnetic heading; minus easterly deviation gives compass heading (add westerly). Variation comes from the earth and is on the chart; deviation comes from the aircraft’s own magnetism and is on the deviation card next to the compass.

ANDS: acceleration errors

Northern hemisphere, on easterly or westerly headings: Accelerate, the compass shows a turn towards North; Decelerate, towards South. Zero on north and south headings, largest on east and west.

UNOS: turning errors

Northern hemisphere: the compass lags when turning through north and leads through south. So Undershoot North (roll out before the compass reaches the heading) and Overshoot South. Largest on north and south, zero through east and west. Both errors reverse in the southern hemisphere.

Gyroscopes: rigidity, precession and drift

A spinning rotor keeps its axis fixed in space (rigidity), and a force on it does not tilt it where you push but 90° further round in the direction of rotation (precession). Fixed in space is not fixed to the earth, which turns under it.

Rigidity

Proportional to the rotor’s moment of inertia and its spin speed. A heavy rotor with its mass at the rim, spinning fast, is hard to disturb. A slowing rotor (loss of its power supply) loses rigidity gradually, so the failure is quiet and the instrument lies slowly. The laser gyros in the A320 have no rotor and no such failure mode.

Real and apparent wander

Real wander is the axis actually moving, from bearing friction and imbalance. Apparent wander is the axis staying fixed while the earth turns under it (earth rate, 15° per hour × sin latitude) and while you fly over a curved, rotating earth (transport wander). The DI has no north-seeking ability, so it must be realigned with the compass, typically every 10 to 15 minutes in steady flight.

Attitude indicator errors

The horizon gyro is kept vertical by an erection system that senses gravity, so it is fooled by sustained accelerations. In the classic instrument, accelerating on take-off gives a small false nose-up and right bank, and prolonged turns leave small pitch and bank errors that slowly erect out afterwards. Older instruments also topple beyond their pitch and bank limits. The A320’s IRs have no erection system and cannot topple.

From gyros to AHRS, IRS and the A320 ADIRS

An inertial reference system has no spinning rotor. Ring laser gyros measure rotation from the time difference between two light beams travelling opposite ways round a closed path (the Sagnac effect), and accelerometers measure acceleration. To start, the system must find north by sensing the earth’s rotation while the aircraft stands still: that is the alignment.
