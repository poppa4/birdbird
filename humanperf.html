Information processing and attention

Everything you know about the state of the aircraft arrives through the senses and then has to pass a narrow bottleneck: attention and working memory. Long-term memory and well-practised skills can run partly outside that bottleneck, which is why an experienced pilot can fly and talk at the same time. That works only until the demand rises above what the bottleneck can carry.

Sensory memory and perception

Each sense holds a raw copy for a moment: about half a second for vision, a few seconds for hearing. That is why you can still catch the end of a call you were not listening to. Perception then gives the input a meaning, and it uses what you expect. A pilot who expects “cleared to land” can hear it in a call that said something else.

Working memory and chunking

Working memory holds roughly 7 ± 2 unrelated items, and they fade in about 20 seconds unless you rehearse them. Chunking stretches the limit: “one two seven decimal eight five five” is six items, “127.855” said as two groups is closer to two. Writing the clearance down is the most reliable chunking of all.

Long-term memory and skills

Long-term memory holds knowledge (semantic), experiences (episodic) and motor programmes (procedural skills). A well-practised skill runs with little attention, which frees capacity. The price is that under stress an old habit can take over, for example the procedure from a previous type.

Situational awareness

A common model has three levels: perceive what is going on (level 1), understand what it means (level 2), and project where it is going (level 3). Spare capacity is what makes level 3 possible. Most losses of awareness start at level 1: the information was there, but nobody looked at it.

Arousal, workload and startle

Performance is best at a medium level of arousal. Too little and attention wanders; too much and attention narrows, working memory shrinks and habits take over. The best level is lower for a complex or unfamiliar task than for a simple, well-practised one. This inverted U is known as the Yerkes–Dodson relationship.

Startle and surprise

Startle is a reflex to a sudden, intense stimulus such as a bang or a loud warning: heart rate jumps, muscles tense, and thinking stops for a few seconds. Surprise is cognitive: what happens does not match what you expected, and you need time to rebuild the picture. Together they can take away capacity for 30 seconds or more, which is a long time at low height.

Managing a startle

Many operators train a short reset: take your hands off anything you were about to do, breathe, look at the attitude and the flight path, say what you see, and only then act. Flying the aircraft first is always right; the checklist can wait a few seconds.

Underload

A long cruise at night is low workload, low arousal and a low point of the body clock at the same time. Vigilance falls, and a slowly developing problem such as a fuel imbalance or a wrong FMS entry can go unnoticed. Planned cross-checks and short conversations about the flight help keep monitoring alive.

Managing workload

Plan and brief in quiet phases, so the busy phases only execute the plan. Prioritise (fly, navigate, communicate, manage), delegate clearly between PF and PM, and choose the level of automation that lowers the workload right now. Ask ATC for time when you need it.

Circadian rhythm, sleep and fatigue

Alertness is shaped by two processes. Sleep pressure builds up for every hour you are awake and is only removed by sleep. The body clock adds a daily wave with its low point in the early morning and a smaller dip after lunch. Plan a day below and see where the duty falls on the curve.

Window of circadian low

EASA flight time limitations define the window of circadian low (WOCL) as 02:00 to 05:59 in the time zone you are acclimatised to. Body temperature and alertness are lowest then, and reaction times are longest. Duties that touch the WOCL get shorter limits.

Sleep debt

Sleeping 5 to 6 hours for a few nights builds a debt that is felt less than it is measured: people consistently rate themselves as more alert than their performance shows. Only sleep pays the debt back, and it usually takes more than one good night. Caffeine hides the sleep pressure for a few hours but does not remove it.

Fatigue compared with alcohol

A well-known study found that after about 17 hours awake, performance on simple tasks was similar to a blood alcohol concentration of about 0.05 %, and after about 24 hours similar to about 0.1 %. Fatigue is an impairment, not a matter of attitude.

Naps and controlled rest

A short nap of about 20 to 30 minutes refreshes without much grogginess. Longer naps reach deep sleep, and waking from it brings sleep inertia: slow thinking for 15 to 30 minutes. A 90-minute nap covers a full sleep cycle and is a good preparation for a night duty. Controlled rest on the flight deck is planned to stay short for the same reason.

Threat and error management and CRM

Threat and error management (TEM) accepts that threats will appear and that crews will make errors. Safety comes from anticipating the threats, catching the errors, and recovering from any undesired aircraft state before it becomes an incident. The three categories are not about blame; they decide what kind of countermeasure works.

Threats

Events or conditions outside the crew’s influence that increase complexity and must be managed: weather, a late runway change, an MEL item, a busy airport, terrain, a similar call sign, a time pressure from the schedule. Some are expected and can be briefed; some arrive without warning; some are latent, built into the system.

Errors

Actions or inactions by the crew that lead away from what was intended: handling errors (wrong FCU selection), procedural errors (a skipped checklist item, a wrong MCDU entry) and communication errors (a misheard clearance). An error that is caught in time has no consequence.

Undesired aircraft states

A position, speed, attitude or configuration that reduces the safety margin: an altitude bust, a speed well above target, an unstable approach, a wrong taxiway or a runway incursion. The aircraft is now in the wrong state, and the first job is to fix the state, not to find out who caused it.

The Swiss-cheese model

Each layer of defence has holes. Latent conditions (a weak roster, an unclear procedure, a training gap) make the holes bigger long before the flight. Active failures by the crew open the last ones. An accident needs the holes to line up. Degrade layers and watch how many hazards get through.

Scenario drill

Eight short A320 situations. First decide whether it is a threat, an error or an undesired aircraft state. Then choose the best countermeasure.

Speaking up: the assertiveness ladder

Night ILS, captain flying. Passing 1500 ft the speed is VAPP + 15 kt and the aircraft is still in CONF 2. At this rate it will not be stabilised by 1000 ft. You are the first officer. Climb the ladder only as far as you need to, and know where the top is before you start.

Briefings that work

A good briefing names the two or three threats of today and says what the crew will do about them: “If we are not stable by 1000 ft, we go around.” Reading the chart aloud is not a briefing. The plan agreed in a quiet phase is what lets either pilot recognise a deviation later.

Cross-check and callouts

Every FCU and MCDU change is announced and checked by the other pilot against the clearance, and FMA changes are called out. The point is not distrust: two people checking the same thing catch errors that one person cannot see in their own work.

Authority gradient

If the gradient is too steep, the first officer does not speak up; if it is too flat, nobody decides. A captain who invites challenge in the briefing (“tell me if you see anything you don’t like”) makes the ladder much shorter for the first officer.

The blind spot

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye there are no receptors. With both eyes open each eye covers the other’s gap, but a windscreen pillar or one closed eye can hide a whole aircraft. Close your left eye, look at the cross with your right eye and move slowly towards or away from the screen. At some distance the dot disappears.

Empty-field myopia

With nothing to focus on, such as a clear blue sky above a haze layer or a dark night, the eye relaxes to a focus about 1 to 2 m away. Distant aircraft then go unseen even when you are looking. Focus briefly on something far away, such as a cloud, the ground or the wingtip, before you scan.

Scanning

The eye only sees sharply in a narrow cone of a few degrees. A useful scan moves in steps of about 10 degrees and stops for about a second in each step, because the eye cannot detect a small target while it is moving. Sweeping the eyes across the sky sees almost nothing.

Cones and rods

Cones in the centre of the eye give sharp, coloured daylight vision. Rods, spread around it, are far more sensitive but colour-blind and have no rods in the very centre. At night an object can disappear when you look straight at it, and reappear when you look slightly to one side.
