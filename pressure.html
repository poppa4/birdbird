1 · Understand

Key concepts

Air has weight, so it presses on everything below it. Differences in that pressure push the air around, and the turning Earth and the ground decide where it really goes. Each card links to the place in the model where it is shown.

Pressure, highs and lows in one diagram, and every term in full
Pressure falls with height
The higher you are, the less air is above you. Near the ground pressure falls about 1 hPa every 27 ft (pilots round it to 30 ft). At about 18 000 ft it has halved.
Station pressure and QFE
The pressure measured at the aerodrome. With QFE set, the altimeter shows height above the aerodrome: zero on the runway.
QNH
Station pressure reduced to mean sea level (MSL) using the standard atmosphere. With QNH set, the altimeter shows altitude above MSL: the aerodrome elevation on the ground.
QFF
Station pressure reduced to MSL using the actual temperature. Meteorologists use it to draw the isobars on surface charts. It can differ from QNH by a few hPa, most at high aerodromes in cold weather.
1013.25 hPa (standard)
Set above the transition altitude, so that all aircraft fly flight levels on the same reference.

Highs and lows. A weather chart joins places with the same MSL pressure by lines called isobars, normally every 4 hPa. A low (depression) has the lowest pressure in the middle: air converges near the ground and rises, so lows bring cloud, rain and stronger wind, and the fronts belong to them. A high (anticyclone) has the highest pressure in the middle: air sinks and warms, so highs bring settled weather and light wind, but in winter they can trap fog, low stratus and haze under an inversion for days. The change of pressure with distance is the pressure gradient: where the isobars are close together, it is steep.

Deep dive

Why about 27 ft per hPa? The weight of a thin layer of air equals the pressure difference across it: Δp = ρ·g·Δh. At sea level ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, so 1 hPa (100 Pa) is 100 / (1.225 × 9.81) = 8.3 m ≈ 27 ft. Higher up the air is thinner, so 1 hPa is a thicker layer: about 36 ft at 10 000 ft, 50 ft at FL180 and more than 70 ft at FL300.

Standard pressure levels (ISA). 850 hPa ≈ FL050, 700 hPa ≈ FL100, 500 hPa ≈ FL180, 300 hPa ≈ FL300, 250 hPa ≈ FL340, 200 hPa ≈ FL390. Upper-air wind and temperature charts are drawn on these levels.

Other shapes on the chart. A ridge is a tongue of high pressure, usually fair weather. A trough is a tongue of low pressure, often with a line of showers or a front. A col is the flat area between two highs and two lows: light and variable wind, with fog in winter or thunderstorms in summer.

Deep dive · the formula behind the numbers

Geostrophic wind: Vg = Δp / (ρ · f · Δn), where Δp/Δn is the pressure gradient, ρ the air density and f = 2Ω sin φ the Coriolis parameter (Ω = 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹, φ = latitude).

Worked example: 4 hPa per 100 NM (400 Pa over 185 200 m) at 60°N with ρ = 1.225 kg/m³: f = 1.26 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹, so Vg = 400 / (1.225 × 1.26 × 10⁻⁴ × 185 200) = 14 m/s ≈ 27 kt. The same gradient at 30°N gives about 47 kt, and at FL100 (ρ ≈ 0.91) about 37 kt.

So for the same isobar spacing the wind is stronger at low latitude and stronger higher up. Near the equator f approaches zero, the balance breaks down and isobars say little about the wind.

    3 · Key points

    What happened, and why it matters

    1Pressure gradient

    Pressure differences push air from high towards low pressure, straight across the isobars. The closer the isobars, the stronger the push. On its own it would accelerate the air into the low.

    2Coriolis

    The Earth turns under the moving air, which turns it right in the northern hemisphere (left in the southern). It grows with wind speed and latitude, and is zero at the equator. When it balances the pressure gradient, the wind blows along the isobars: the geostrophic wind.

    3Friction

    Below about 2000–3000 ft the ground slows the wind. Slower wind means weaker Coriolis, so the pressure gradient wins a little and the wind crosses the isobars towards the low: more over rough land, less over the smooth sea.

    Pilot takeaways

    • The wind veers and increases with height through the friction layer in the northern hemisphere. Climbing out, expect it to turn clockwise and pick up; on the approach it backs and drops. In the southern hemisphere the turn is the other way.
    • The surface wind backs (NH) and slows: over land about 30° and roughly half the 2000 ft wind, over the sea about 10–15° and roughly 70 %.
    • Tight isobars mean strong wind, gusts, mechanical turbulence and strong crosswind near the ground, and wind shear at the top of the friction layer.
    • High to low, look out below. Flying towards a low without updating the QNH, the altimeter reads too high: about 27 ft per hPa. Set the new QNH as soon as you get it. In the northern hemisphere the wind then comes from the left, so you drift right.
    • Local winds can beat the chart. A sea breeze can turn the runway wind round by the afternoon, katabatic wind brings early-morning tailwind and turbulence to valley and fjord airports, and föhn brings strong, gusty and turbulent wind on the lee side of mountains.
    Deep dive

    Curved isobars: the gradient wind. Around a low part of the pressure force is used to turn the air, so the real wind is weaker than geostrophic (subgeostrophic). Around a high it is stronger (supergeostrophic), but highs have weak gradients. This model ignores curvature, so it overestimates the wind close to a tight low.

    Diurnal variation. By day, heating mixes the faster wind aloft down to the surface: over land the surface wind veers and increases in the afternoon. At night the air near the ground becomes stable and decouples: the surface wind backs and drops, and the wind just above the inversion can be much stronger (a low-level jet and wind shear at night).

    Higher up. Pressure gradients change with height because cold air is denser than warm air. Where cold and warm air meet, the gradient aloft grows with height and the wind strengthens up to the tropopause: the jet streams. See Fronts and air masses.
