Cabin altitude and differential pressure

Bleed air from the engines goes through the air conditioning packs into the cabin, and an outflow valve lets it out again. By closing the valve the controller holds the air in and keeps the cabin pressure higher than outside. The difference is the differential pressure (ΔP), and the fuselage is built for a maximum of it. The higher you fly, the higher the cabin has to go.

Limits on ΔP

On the A320 family the normal maximum differential pressure is about 8 psi, and safety valves open at about 8.6 psi (typical values). At FL390 the outside pressure is under 3 psi, so 8 psi of ΔP gives a cabin of about 8000 ft. That is the design trade: a stronger, heavier fuselage could keep the cabin lower.

Cabin rate

Ears feel the rate of change, not the altitude. The controller spreads the cabin climb over the whole aircraft climb, and the cabin descent over the whole descent, so the rates stay at a few hundred feet per minute even when the aircraft climbs or descends at 2000 to 3000 ft/min.

Negative ΔP

The fuselage is not built to have higher pressure outside than inside. If the aircraft descends faster than the cabin can follow, negative relief valves open and let air in. Normally the controller prevents this by keeping the cabin a little below the aircraft path.

Ditching

The DITCHING pushbutton closes the outflow valve and the openings below the floor line, so water does not flood the fuselage through the valves after a water landing.

Decompression and emergency descent

A leak is a hole the packs have to fill. Small holes are absorbed: the outflow valve closes a little more and nobody notices. A larger hole beats the packs and the cabin climbs; a big one empties the cabin in seconds. The size of the hole compared with the cabin volume decides how fast.

Explosive, rapid and slow

Teaching definitions vary, but roughly: explosive decompression is over in less than about half a second, faster than the lungs can empty; rapid takes from a few seconds to tens of seconds; slow, or gradual, takes long enough that it may not be noticed without the warnings. Rapid decompression comes with a bang, fog as the air cools, flying debris and a strong outflow of air towards the hole. In this lab anything up to about a minute counts as rapid, and for it the time of useful consciousness is roughly halved.

The emergency descent

Masks on and communication established first. Then descend: thrust idle, speedbrakes out, and fly at MMO and then VMO (unless structural damage is suspected, when a lower speed is wiser), towards 10,000 ft or the minimum safe altitude if that is higher. Most of the height is lost in the first few minutes.

Hypoxia and time of useful consciousness

The air holds about 21 % oxygen at every altitude. What falls is the pressure, and with it the partial pressure of oxygen that pushes oxygen from the lungs into the blood. Above about 10,000 ft the blood starts to carry noticeably less, and above 20,000 ft the time you can act sensibly is measured in minutes, then seconds.

Time of useful consciousness

TUC is the time from the loss of oxygen until you can no longer act effectively, not until you pass out. You may still be awake and feel fine after it has run out. After a rapid decompression it is roughly halved, because the lungs dump their oxygen at once and you start from a lower level.
