Reference

Phraseology

Always read back

Route clearances. Clearances and instructions to enter, land on, take off from, hold short of, cross, taxi on or backtrack a runway. Runway in use, altimeter settings (QNH), squawk codes, new frequencies, level, heading and speed instructions, and transition levels, also those in the ATIS (SERA.8015(e)(1)). Other clearances, such as conditional clearances and taxi instructions, are read back or acknowledged so it is clear you understood them (SERA.8015(e)(2)). Include your callsign.

First call

Station called, your full callsign, then where you are and what you want (SERA.14055(b)(1)). After a frequency change also give your level, with the passing and cleared level if you are climbing or descending, and any speed ATC gave you (SERA.14065). Example: “Birdbird Tower, LN-NAE, DA42, 10 miles north, 2000 feet, information Bravo, request joining for landing.”

Conditional clearances

Callsign, condition, clearance, condition again (SERA.8015(ec)). Acknowledge the same way: “Behind the landing DA40, lining up runway 36, behind.” Only when you and the controller can both see the traffic: identify it before you move.

Words to avoid

Say “ready for departure”: “take-off” is only used in the take-off clearance or to cancel it (SERA.14045(c)). “Say again”, not “repeat”. “Affirm”, not “yes” or “affirmative”. “Roger” is never a readback or an answer to a question. A hold short is acknowledged with “holding short”. “QNH”, not “altimeter”. No “over and out”.

Levels

Climb or descend “to” the level (AMC1 SERA.14001, 1.1.2). Below the transition altitude: “3000 feet” on QNH, “altitude 3000 feet” where that helps. At or above the transition level: “flight level 90” on 1013 hPa, and “flight level one hundred” for whole hundreds. Never mix the two.

Callsign abbreviation

LN-NAE may become L-AE or L-NAE (first character and at least the last two), but only after the station has used the short form, and never when you call a new unit. Callsigns with a flight number are never abbreviated (SERA.14050, SERA.14055).

Numbers

Each digit separately, “decimal” for the point. Exceptions (SERA.14035): altitudes in whole hundreds and thousands (“two thousand five hundred”), “flight level one hundred”, “QNH one thousand”, “squawk seven thousand”, “ten o’clock”. Frequencies use all six digits, but only four when the last two are zeros: 118.300 is “one one eight decimal three”, 119.250 is “one one niner decimal two five zero”.

Emergencies

MAYDAY (distress) or PAN PAN (urgency), preferably three times, then station, callsign, nature, intention, position, level and heading (SERA.14095). MINIMUM FUEL is not an emergency; MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY FUEL is (SERA.11012). Radio failure: squawk 7600, and with a receiver failure transmit blind and repeat the message (SERA.14083, SERA.14085).

Norway

What is different at home

Transition altitude

7000 ft at almost every aerodrome in AIP Norway AD 2; 5000 ft at Svalbard (Longyear and Ny-Ålesund). The transition level depends on the QNH and comes in the ATIS or from ATC. Climbing, set 1013 after passing the transition altitude; descending, set QNH after passing the transition level (ENR 1.7).

AFIS aerodromes

An AFIS unit is called “(aerodrome) INFORMATION”, e.g. “Valan Information”. It gives information, not clearances. Read back the runway information, QNH and transition level, any squawk, and any ATC clearance it relays. Tell AFIS if you will not use the runway in use. The TIZ around it is radio mandatory: call before you enter (GEN 3.3 4.1).

Runway information

“No reported traffic runway 18”: no aircraft, vehicles or people are using the runway. “Runway 18 occupied”: someone will use it or is using it, so stay off it unless it is coordinated. Neither is a clearance, and both are read back (GEN 3.3 4.1.4–4.1.5). Where one AFIS officer serves several aerodromes, the aerodrome name is said in every runway message and you hear the other aerodrome too (AIC A 01/2026).

Uncontrolled fields

Without a ground station you transmit blind to “… Traffic” on the published frequency, say your intentions, and report downwind, base and final, as AD 2 describes for such fields. Saying the field name again at the end is UK practice: useful, not required.

Light signals from AFIS

A series of green flashes means “return for landing”, and no steady green follows. Red flashes: aerodrome unserviceable. Red pyrotechnic: landing not advisable, make another circuit (GEN 3.3 4.1.6). At a controlled aerodrome the SERA signals apply.
